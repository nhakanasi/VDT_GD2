\begin{abstract}
Text-to-speech (TTS) systems can read Vietnamese aloud, but they cannot yet reliably make
non-verbal sounds, such as laughing, sighing or coughing, at the point where the text asks for
them. These sounds make synthetic speech feel human in voice assistants, customer-facing AI
agents, audiobooks and videos. Two things stand in the way: existing systems ignore or garble
such tags in Vietnamese, and no Vietnamese training data with labelled sounds exists. We build
that data automatically by adapting the SynParaSpeech method to Vietnamese: recordings of real
sounds are converted into each speaker's voice and inserted into natural pauses in real
Vietnamese speech, with a matching tag in the transcript. The result is about 811 hours of
labelled speech covering 17 sound types. Fine-tuning the open-source OmniVoice model on this
data teaches it 15 new tags. The fine-tuned model makes the right sound at the right place for
50\% of tags, against 11.5\% for the released model, and misses only 35\% of them, against 64\%.
Its word error rate on the tagged sentences falls from 8.1\% to 6.7\%; on plain text it rises a
little, from 3.9\% to 4.8\%, with the same speaker similarity, and it runs at a real-time factor
of 0.06, fast enough for live conversation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
When people talk, they communicate with more than words. A short laugh, a sigh, a breath before
a long sentence or a hesitant ``\viet{ừm}\ldots'' tells the listener how the speaker feels and
what they mean. These paralinguistic, or non-verbal, cues, which we call non-verbal
vocalizations (NVVs), run through everyday conversation, and speech without them sounds flat and
mechanical even when every word is pronounced correctly.

Text-to-speech (TTS) systems turn written text into spoken audio. Modern TTS models read text
clearly in many voices, and some now let the user place a tag in the text, as in
``\viet{Thật à [laughter] mình không ngờ luôn}'' (``Really? [laughter] I didn't expect that at
all''), so that the voice laughs at exactly that point. This control is what makes synthetic
speech usable where expression matters. A voice agent that answers customers can chuckle at a
joke or sigh in sympathy at a complaint, instead of meeting both in the same even tone, and
that small reaction is often what makes a caller feel heard rather than processed. An
audiobook, a dubbed video or a podcast can let its narrator gasp, laugh or catch a breath where
the script calls for it, which holds a listener's attention far better than a steady,
unbroken read.

For Vietnamese, this control does not exist yet. What is needed is a TTS system that, given a
Vietnamese sentence with a tag such as \texttt{[cough]}, makes that sound at that point in the
sentence, in the chosen voice, as naturally as a person would. To our knowledge, no available
system can do this. Systems that accept such tags are built mainly for other languages: Fish
Audio's open model, OpenAudio S1~\citep{openaudios1}, offers markers such as
\texttt{(laughing)} and \texttt{(sighing)} in 13 languages, none of them Vietnamese.
OmniVoice~\citep{omnivoice} does speak Vietnamese, but its tags were learned from Chinese and
English data, and in Vietnamese it often ignores them, reads them out as words or produces
broken audio around them. In our tests on Vietnamese sentences, the released OmniVoice made no
sound at all for 64\% of the tags, and the right sound in the right place for only 11.5\%
(\S\ref{sec:results}). The natural remedy, training a model on Vietnamese speech in which each
sound is labelled at its position, is blocked by a lack of data: to our knowledge, no public
Vietnamese dataset of this kind exists.

This work closes both gaps. We start from OmniVoice, an open-source TTS model that already
speaks Vietnamese and runs many times faster than real time, which makes it practical to deploy
in live conversation. Of the sounds we need, it supports only laughter and sigh, so we teach it
15 new tags: cough, throat clearing, sneeze, sniff, breathing, gasp, moan, grunt, burp, yawn,
crying, pant, yell, screaming and snoring. Because no suitable data existed, we build it by
adapting SynParaSpeech~\citep{synparaspeech}, a published method that creates such data
automatically by inserting recorded sounds into real speech, to Vietnamese, and we extend it in
five ways. We collect 17 sound types instead of 6, keeping only clean recordings from several
public collections. An audio language model listens to each recording and places the tag
among the speaker's real pauses, where SynParaSpeech uses a text-only model that reads the
transcript. Each candidate recording of the sound is scored on how well its loudness, recording
quality and rhythm match the speaker, where SynParaSpeech picks one at random. The inserted
sound is set to a suitable level and given as much silence as the speaker normally pauses for,
filled with the recording's own background noise. Finally, every example is scored by an
automatic quality predictor and dropped if the edit damaged the audio, where SynParaSpeech
relies on manual review. We then fine-tune OmniVoice on the resulting dataset, that is, train
it further on our data.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item A Vietnamese NVV data pipeline that adapts SynParaSpeech to Vietnamese and extends it with
a larger sound inventory, audio-aware tag placement, speaker-matched clip selection, natural
pauses and automatic quality filtering (\S\ref{sec:data}).
\item A Vietnamese NVV dataset of about 811 hours (224,064 utterances) covering 17 sound types,
with 10,000 to 13,500 utterances per type and the position of each sound labelled in the
transcript.
\item A fine-tuned OmniVoice that makes the right sound at the right place for 50\% of tags,
against 11.5\% for the released model, and lowers the word error rate on tagged sentences from
8.1\% to 6.7\%, at a small cost on plain text (WER 4.8\% against 3.9\%, with the same speaker
similarity), while staying fast, with a real-time factor (the time taken to generate speech
divided by the length of that speech) of 0.06, the same as the released model.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}
Our system extends a pretrained zero-shot TTS model with control over non-verbal
vocalizations (NVV) in Vietnamese. Since Vietnamese speech with NVV annotations is scarce, we
synthesize training data by inserting voice-converted NVV clips into real Vietnamese recordings
at natural pauses, with an inline tag at the matching position in the transcript
(\S\ref{sec:data}). We then fine-tune the TTS model on this data mixed with untagged Vietnamese
speech (\S\ref{sec:adapt}). At inference, an inline tag such as \texttt{[laughter]} in the input
text produces the corresponding vocalization in the cloned voice. Figure~\ref{fig:overview}
gives an overview.

\subsection{Training-data synthesis}\label{sec:data}
Each training example is a real Vietnamese utterance with one NVV event spliced into one of its
pauses and a tag inserted at the same point in its transcript. It is built from a pool of NVV
clips, collected once, in six steps per utterance (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}a).
Appendix~\ref{app:data} gives the settings of each part.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{NVV clips.} Built once, before synthesis. VocalSound~\citep{vocalsound} alone is
limited: it covers six classes
(laughter, sigh, cough, throat clearing, sneeze, sniff), and its crowdsourced clips often carry
long silences or stray speech from a loose cut. To add classes and keep only clean events, we
pool clips from other NVV corpora and from events cut out of a tagged speech corpus. Where a
source labels whole clips or whole captions
rather than events, the events are first located in time. Every event is then trimmed and kept
only if it is a clean, isolated instance of its class: a sound event detector confirms its class
and no other, no speech overlaps it, its length is plausible for the class, and it is neither
buried in noise nor clipped. The pool covers 17 classes (Table~\ref{tab:nvv}).

\item \textbf{Pause detection.} Following SynParaSpeech~\citep{synparaspeech}, we first find the
pauses where an NVV could go and only then let a model choose among them. Placing the tag first
and aligning it afterwards fails on connected speech, where the boundary between two words is
usually continuous voicing. A voice activity detector finds the pauses, a forced aligner finds
the word boundaries, and a pause that falls between two words becomes a \emph{slot}. The slots
are marked with numbered markers in the transcript, e.g.\
``\viet{\ldots\ chứ thôi , <1> để nó lành thôi \ldots}'' in Figure~\ref{fig:overview}a.

\item \textbf{Tag selection.} An omni-modal LLM listens to the recording, reads the marked
transcript, and picks the one slot where a non-verbal sound would most plausibly occur,
together with the tag that fits what the speaker is doing there, or declines if no slot suits
any sound. The LLM never writes transcript text: the tag is inserted in code at the chosen slot
and the event is later spliced at the slot's midpoint, so text and audio refer to the same point
by construction. Left alone, the LLM settles on a few neutral classes, so a tag balancer
withholds any tag that already holds too large a share of the assignments until the others
catch up.

\item \textbf{Clip selection.} Within the chosen class, a clip is drawn at random, weighted towards
clips that match the host speaker on the properties that voice conversion leaves unchanged.
Timbre and pitch are rebuilt by the conversion, so we do not match on them. Instead we compare
the clip with the speech just before the cut on four measures: \emph{intensity},
\emph{clarity} (the recording's signal-to-noise ratio), \emph{tempo} (for laughter only) and
\emph{tilt} (whether the energy rises or decays). With each distance $d_a$ normalised by a
per-measure tolerance and weighted by $\lambda_a$, a clip is drawn with probability
proportional to
\begin{equation}
w = \exp\Big(-\sum_a \lambda_a d_a\Big).
\end{equation}
Sampling, rather than taking the closest clip, keeps every usable clip in play, so a
soft-spoken speaker still sometimes gets a loud laugh.

\item \textbf{Voice conversion.} The selected clip is converted to the host speaker's voice with
Seed-VC, using the host utterance itself as the voice reference. Pitch conditioning is off,
since its pitch tracker is built for speech and singing and fails on coughs, sniffs and throat
clearing.

\item \textbf{Splicing.} The converted event is scaled to the loudness of the speech before the
cut, with a per-class offset (a sneeze is louder than a sniff), and spliced in at the slot's
midpoint with short exponential fades, which keep the sharp onset of a laugh or cough that a
cosine fade would soften. Each side of the event should then carry about as much silence as
the speaker normally pauses for. The target pauses are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
T_{\mathrm{pre}} &= \bar{T}_{p}\cdot s_g\cdot s_{\mathrm{pre}}^{(c)}\\
T_{\mathrm{post}} &= \bar{T}_{p}\cdot s_g\cdot s_{\mathrm{post}}^{(c)}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{T}_{p}$ is the speaker's median pause in the utterance, $s_g$ is a global scale,
and $s_{\mathrm{pre}}^{(c)}$ and $s_{\mathrm{post}}^{(c)}$ are scales for class $c$: events
that end on emptied lungs, such as a laugh or a sigh, need a longer beat after them. Silence is
only added up to the target, so the speaker's own pause is never shortened, and the added
silence is filled with the recording's own background noise.

\item \textbf{Filter.} Each finished example is scored with NISQA~v2.0~\citep{nisqa}, a
no-reference predictor of the mean opinion score (MOS) and of four quality dimensions, on both
the source recording and the spliced one. It is kept only if neither its overall quality nor
its continuity drops by more than a threshold. The thresholds apply to the drop rather than to
absolute scores, because an absolute floor mostly measures how clean the source corpus was. A
further check rejects cuts that land inside loud speech, which NISQA no longer hears once the
fades and the added silence have smoothed the junction.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Fine-tuning and inference}\label{sec:adapt}
We build on OmniVoice~\citep{omnivoice}, a massively multilingual zero-shot TTS model that maps
text directly to multi-codebook acoustic tokens with a non-autoregressive diffusion language
model and clones a voice from a reference recording. The released model cannot generate
Vietnamese NVVs as it stands, for three reasons. First, its tag set is small: only two of its
inline tags, \texttt{[laughter]} and \texttt{[sigh]}, are non-verbal sounds, the rest being
lexical interjections such as \texttt{[question-ah]}, so 15 of our 17 classes have no tag at
all. Second, it has never seen a tag in Vietnamese: Vietnamese speech, ViVoice among it, is in
its training data but untagged, and its tagged data come from Chinese and English corpora
(NVSpeech, NVS-38K and ParaSpeechCaps).% TODO cite NVSpeech, ParaSpeechCaps
\ Third, it was not trained for tag control: these corpora are a small fraction of its training
data, and the OmniVoice paper does not evaluate tag control. In practice the released model
often drops the tag in Vietnamese text, even for the two classes it supports
(\S\ref{sec:results}).

We therefore fine-tune OmniVoice on the synthetic data of \S\ref{sec:data}, which supplies all
17 tags on Vietnamese speech, mixed with untagged Vietnamese speech to preserve ordinary
synthesis. Nothing else changes: the training objective is OmniVoice's own, and at inference
the tag is written inline in the input text, as with OmniVoice's own tags.

For zero-shot TTS, every system, the four Vietnamese systems included, is evaluated on the same
238 utterances sampled from VietVoice, an internal Vietnamese speech dataset,% TODO: say how each utterance's reference recording is chosen

For NVV synthesis, we use 420 real Vietnamese sentences, 30 for each of 14 classes; burp, grunt
and yell are not tested. Each sentence is chosen so that the tag fits its content, for example
a sentence in which someone bursts out laughing for \texttt{[laughter]}, and the tag sits where
the sound would naturally occur (Appendix~\ref{app:test}). Each sentence is synthesised once with
each of 7 reference voices, prompted with a reference recording and its transcript. None of the
7 voices appears in the training data, so every result is for a voice the model has never
heard.% CHECK: confirm none of the 7 reference voices is a ViVoice speaker.
\ Each system therefore produces 2,940 items (420 sentences $\times$ 7 voices). Every objective
metric is computed over all items of its task; the listening test uses a subset of the NVV
items.

Both OmniVoice models use its voice-cloning inference with the language set to Vietnamese, and
the same item gets the same random seed in both. The released inference code tokenises
an inline tag apart from the text around it, whereas training tokenises the whole string, so a
model can miss or garble tags it has learned. We therefore tokenise the whole string at
inference for both models, the released one included, so that they receive the same input.

We report five objective metrics, each on its own as the mean over all items of a system unless
stated otherwise; no combined score is computed. NVPA, WER and SIM-o follow Track~A of the
VLSP~2026 NVV TTS task,% TODO cite
\ and Appendix~\ref{app:metrics} gives the full procedures and model versions.
% TODO cite FlexSED, ECAPA-TDNN, Zipformer.

NV placement accuracy (NVPA) measures whether the tagged vocalization is produced where the tag
sits. A sound event detector (FlexSED) finds the vocalizations in the output, and a Vietnamese
CTC forced aligner places the tag among the spoken words; a tag counts as correct when a
vocalization of its class is found at its position. NVPA is the share of correct tags, from 0 to
1, where 1 means every tag became the right sound in the right place. The NV miss rate (NVMR) is
the share of tags at which no vocalization of any class is found, because the model skipped the
tag or read it out as words. NVPA also counts a wrong or misplaced vocalization as a failure, so
NVMR isolates the tags that were ignored; it also runs from 0 to 1, and lower is better.

Word error rate (WER) measures intelligibility: a Vietnamese ASR model (Zipformer) transcribes
the output, and the transcript is compared with the input text, tags removed. It is the share of
words substituted, deleted or inserted, in percent; lower is better, 0\% means every word was
recognised, and it can exceed 100\% when many words are inserted. Speaker similarity (SIM-o)
measures how well the voice is cloned, as the cosine between the speaker embeddings (ECAPA-TDNN)
of the output and of the reference recording used as the prompt. It ranges from $-1$ to 1, in
practice from 0 to 1, and values near 1 mean the output sounds like the reference speaker. The
real-time factor (RTF) measures speed: the time taken to synthesise an utterance divided by the
duration of the audio produced, on one NVIDIA A100 GPU (DGX A100). Lower is better, and below 1
is faster than real time; an RTF of 0.5, for example, produces 10\,s of speech in 5\,s. We report
the mean over items.

The listening test covers NVV synthesis only. Native Vietnamese listeners rate samples from
three systems, Fish Audio, the released OmniVoice and our fine-tuned model, hearing one sample
at a time and giving it three mean opinion scores (MOS) on a five-point scale (1 = bad,
5 = excellent; higher is better). MOS-Q rates quality: how clean the audio is, free of noise,
distortion and glitches. MOS-N rates naturalness: how much the speech sounds like a real person
talking, including whether the vocalization sounds real and sits naturally in the sentence.
MOS-E rates expressiveness: how well the sample conveys the reaction the text calls for, that
is, whether the vocalization is the one the tag asks for and carries the feeling of the
sentence, rather than sounding flat or out of place. Ten listeners each rate 40 samples,
balanced across the three systems, and we report each score as a mean with its 95\% confidence
interval (Table~\ref{tab:mos}).

\subsection{Results}\label{sec:results}

model's (0.74 against 0.75). On plain text without tags (Table~\ref{tab:zeroshot}), the cost is
small: the word error rate rises from 3.93\% to 4.78\%, and speaker similarity is the same, 0.84
for both models.

subset of the NVV test set it misses 65\% of the tags and realises only 23\% correctly, against
35\% and 50\% for our model, with a word error rate of 60.48\%.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
We set out to give Vietnamese TTS the ability to laugh, sigh, cough and make other non-verbal
sounds where the text asks for them. The released OmniVoice cannot do this: on our Vietnamese
test sentences it made no sound at all for 64\% of the tags and the right sound in the right
place for only 11.5\%. Because no Vietnamese training data for this task existed, we built a
pipeline that creates it automatically from real Vietnamese speech, adapting SynParaSpeech with
a larger sound inventory, an audio model that places each tag in a real pause, speaker-matched
sound selection, natural pauses around the inserted sound and automatic quality checks. The
pipeline produced about 811 hours of labelled speech covering 17 sound types. Fine-tuned on this
data, OmniVoice makes the right sound at the right place for 50\% of tags, misses 35\% instead of
64\%, and reads tagged sentences more accurately (WER 6.7\% against 8.1\%), at a small cost on plain
text (WER 4.8\% against 3.9\%, with the same speaker similarity) and with a real-time factor of
0.06, fast enough for live conversation. Listeners rate its naturalness and expressiveness well
above both the released model and Fish Audio.

The work has limits that the next steps should address. Each training example carries one sound,
inserted into an existing pause, so sounds that overlap speech, such as laughing while talking,
are not covered. The inserted sounds are recorded by other people and only converted into the
target voice, so their style may not always suit a Vietnamese speaker. Three of the 17 sound
types (burp, grunt and yell) are trained but not yet tested, and several detection thresholds
in the evaluation are still to be calibrated by listening. Next, we plan to train a version on
ViVoice alone to separate the effect of the NVV data from that of extra Vietnamese training, and to
extend the test set to all 17 sound types.

\subsection{NVV clips}
% Code: ../VocalSoundExtend (nvv/pipeline.py, nvv/nvs38k.py, nvv/fsd50k.py),
% ../Para/scripts/extract_nonspeech7k.py and ../Para/scripts/build_clip_index.py.
Besides VocalSound~\citep{vocalsound}, the pool draws on two kinds of source: (1)~other NVV
corpora: Nonspeech7k~\citep{nonspeech7k}, whose clips we use as they are, and
FSD50K~\citep{fsd50k}, whose clip-level labels we localise in time; and (2)~NVV events cut from
an existing tagged speech corpus, NonVerbalSpeech-38K (NVS-38K)~\citep{nvs38k}. From Nonspeech7k
we take breathing, crying, screaming and yawn; its other classes are already covered by
VocalSound. For VocalSound we keep only the test and validation splits (\S\ref{sec:exp-data}).
The pool holds 5,446 VocalSound clips, 4,773 from Nonspeech7k, 1,520 from NVS-38K and 1,426
from FSD50K (Table~\ref{tab:nvv}).

Each source locates its events differently. For NVS-38K, whose captions carry one inline
tag, we force-align the caption words with Qwen3-ForcedAligner. The aligner tends to stretch
the neighbouring words over the non-verbal sound, so the gap between the two words around the
tag does not give the event's extent. It only confirms that the tag refers to the dataset's
own detected non-verbal region (within 1\,s), and that region is what we cut. FSD50K has
clip-level labels only, so the frame posterior of FlexSED~\citep{flexsed} for the class prompt
locates the events (frames ${\ge}\,0.5$, gaps up to 0.3\,s merged). FlexSED is an
open-vocabulary sound event detector that takes the class as a text prompt, through CLAP text
embeddings, so one model can locate and check all 17 classes, including those outside any fixed
label set. Clips that carry two of our target labels are skipped, since their events could not
be told apart.

Every event located in FSD50K or NVS-38K is then cut and checked in the same way. Its edges are
tightened to the frame energy within $\pm$0.2\,s of the region, then padded by 75\,ms and faded
by 10\,ms. The event is kept only if (i)~FlexSED scores its class ${\ge}\,0.5$ over the sound
and scores no VocalSound class higher, so a new class does not duplicate an existing one;
(ii)~no other labelled sound of the recording scores ${\ge}\,0.5$ over it; (iii)~Qwen3-ASR finds
no lexical word in it, so no speech overlaps it; (iv)~its length is within a per-class range
(e.g. 0.2--2.5\,s for breathing, 0.8--4.0\,s for a yawn); (v)~its SNR against the recording's
noise floor is at least 6\,dB; and (vi)~at most 0.1\% of its samples are clipped. Nonspeech7k
clips are used whole, without these checks. Table~\ref{tab:nvv} lists the resulting 17 classes.

\subsection{Pause detection}
Placing the tag first and aligning it afterwards fails on this speech. On the pilot, the cut it
gave landed on speech nearly as loud as the utterance's average (median boundary activity 0.85,
see Filter), and the nearest real pause was a median 2.02\,s away from the aligned tag. Moving
the cut to a pause within 0.5\,s worked for 1 of 17 utterances, and within 2.0\,s for only 7 of
17, by which point the event no longer sits between the words the transcript says.

Silero VAD~\citep{silerovad} finds the speech regions. We use a trained speech/non-speech
classifier rather than an energy threshold because a breath in a pause is not silent but is not
speech either; an energy detector also blurs speech by about one 128\,ms analysis frame into the
pause. Regions less than 0.10\,s apart are merged, so stop closures and glottal breaks do not
count as pauses, and the remaining gaps are trimmed by 0.05\,s at each edge and kept if at least
0.12\,s long. Silence before the first word and after the last is ignored.

ViVoice transcripts have no word timing, so Qwen3-ForcedAligner~\citep{qwen3asr} aligns the
untagged transcript to the audio. It needs no pronunciation dictionary, which matters for
Vietnamese conversational fillers such as ``\viet{à}'', ``\viet{ha}'' and ``\viet{thì}'', exactly
the words a tag is spliced next to. The dictionary-based Montreal Forced Aligner~\citep{mfa},
which we also tried, marks such fillers as out-of-vocabulary speech noise and only guesses their
timing. A pause becomes a slot when it overlaps the gap between two aligned words; if two pauses
fall in one gap, the longer is kept. Each slot records its midpoint and the character offset of
the word after it. If any word cannot be matched back to the transcript in order, the utterance
gets no slots and is skipped, rather than risk a tag between the wrong two words. On the pilot,
16 of 20 utterances had at least one slot, 47 slots in all (a median of 2 per utterance) with
pauses of 0.20--1.20\,s; the other 4 had no qualifying pause and were dropped.

\subsection{Tag selection}
The recording and the marked transcript go to Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B-Instruct~\citep{qwen3omni},
served locally with vLLM. An audio model is needed because a numbered position means little from
text alone: listening is what tells a breath pause from a hesitation, and whether a laugh or a
sigh fits the speaker's mood at that point. SynParaSpeech, by contrast, places tags with a
text-only model. Among open models, Qwen3-Omni reports state-of-the-art results on 32 of 36
audio and audio-visual benchmarks~\citep{qwen3omni}, and serving it locally keeps the recordings
on our own servers, with no external API.

The model is asked to listen to the delivery and answer in JSON with the one marker where a
non-verbal sound would most plausibly occur and the one tag from the offered list that fits what
the speaker is doing there, or \texttt{\{"slot": null\}} if no position suits any sound. We
sample with temperature 0.2 and top-$p$ 0.95. An invalid reply is retried, up to three attempts
in all, and a null answer skips the utterance.

Left alone, the model settles on a few classes: on the pilot it tagged 16 utterances, all with
laughter (9) or sigh (7). The tag balancer keeps a running count of the tags assigned so far,
carried across runs, and withholds from the offered list any tag that holds more than 25\% of
them, with the share taken over at least 20 tags, so a tag is first withheld on its sixth pick
within the first 20. A reply that names a withheld tag is re-asked, and the tag is offered again
once the other tags catch up. With the balancer, the training set holds between 10,190 and
13,522 utterances per tag (Table~\ref{tab:synth}).

\subsection{Clip selection}
Clips whose peak is below 0.05 or that are shorter than 0.35\,s after trimming silence, and
clips with more than 1\% of samples at full scale, are excluded. The four measures match the
speaker only on properties that survive voice conversion, which we checked on converted output.
Seed-VC rebuilds pitch from the target speaker: regressing converted F0 on source and speaker F0
gives a source coefficient of $+0.04$ against $0.84$ for the speaker, and source and converted
pitch contours correlate at only $+0.14$. It keeps the recording channel (clip SNR spans
33--60\,dB from the 10th to the 90th percentile, against 42--55\,dB for the speech), the tempo
(envelope rate changes by a median 0.147 octave) and the intensity (crest factor changes by a
median 0.06 octave). Matching on pitch would therefore be wasted effort.

The four measures are taken on each trimmed clip and on the 2.5\,s of speech before the cut:
\emph{intensity}, the mean of the clip's crest-factor and duration ranks within its class,
against the rank of the context window's RMS among all windows of that length in the utterance;
\emph{clarity}, the SNR as the ratio of the 90th to the 10th percentile of 20\,ms frame RMS
(0--60\,dB, measured on the whole utterance for the speaker); \emph{tempo}, envelope peaks per
second, compared in octaves and used for laughter only, since the timing of a cough or sneeze
has nothing to do with speaking rate; and \emph{tilt}, the slope of a line fitted to the log
envelope, in dB from start to end. Each distance is divided by a tolerance set to about a third
of its measured spread (0.35 rank, 12\,dB, 1 octave, 18\,dB). The weights are
$\lambda = 1.0, 1.5, 1.0, 0.5$ for intensity, clarity, tempo and tilt. Clarity weighs most
because VocalSound's microphones vary far more than its speakers' loudness. Tilt has half weight
because its premise, that a speaker trailing off should be followed by a decaying event, has not
been confirmed by listening. So that a soft-spoken speaker still sometimes gets a loud laugh, the
intensity target is drawn per row from $\mathcal{N}(r, 0.2^2)$, clipped to $[0,1]$, around the
speaker's own rank $r$. Clip features are precomputed once for the whole pool, so every row
samples from the full class. On the pilot, against uniform sampling (400 draws per utterance),
weighted sampling lowered the mean mismatch of the chosen clip by 38\% on clarity, 35\% on tempo
and 5\% on intensity, which had the least room to improve.

\subsection{Voice conversion}
Seed-VC~\citep{seedvc} is a zero-shot voice converter, so the host utterance itself can serve as
the voice reference; SynParaSpeech uses it for the same step. The selected clip is trimmed of
silence, peak-normalised to 0.95 and zero-padded to at least 1\,s, as Seed-VC's input chunking
requires. It is converted with Seed-VC~v1 (50 diffusion steps, classifier-free guidance rate 0.7,
length adjustment 1.0, F0 conditioning off) at 22.05\,kHz, and is trimmed again afterwards. F0
conditioning is off because its pitch tracker, RMVPE, is built for speech and singing and fails
on coughs, sniffs and throat clearing.

Each conversion is checked with CAM++~\citep{campplus}, the speaker encoder Seed-VC itself
conditions on. On the pilot, the similarity of the converted event to the utterance had a median
of 0.41, against 0.07 for the unconverted clip, and every conversion beat its clip. A
WavLM-based check, tried first, rated the unconverted clip as closer to the speaker on several
rows (0.539 against 0.410 for the converted event on one of them), so it was dropped. The score
is kept as a diagnostic, not a filter, since a 1--3\,s non-speech sound gives a weaker embedding
than an utterance.

\subsection{Splicing}
The converted event's RMS is set to the RMS of the 2.5\,s of speech before the cut plus a
per-class offset (laughter $+3$\,dB, sneeze $+4$, cough $+2$, throat clearing $-2$, sigh $-4$,
sniff $-5$, and 0 for the other classes), and its peak is capped at 1.5 times the speech's
99.9th-percentile level. Referencing the local RMS rather than the utterance's peak means a
single loud consonant elsewhere cannot set the level: in a test, one stray full-scale sample
moved a peak-referenced event by $+10.4$\,dB and a context-referenced one by 0\,dB. The recording
is cut at the slot's midpoint, and a 50\,ms exponential fade (curvature $k{=}5$) is applied to
all four edges: the end of the speech before the cut, both ends of the event and the start of
the speech after it. An exponential fade reaches half level within the first 14\% of its length,
which keeps the sharp onset of a laugh, cough or sneeze. On the pilot, the largest
sample-to-sample jump across the cut fell from a mean of 0.00163 with plain concatenation to
0.00012, smaller on 15 of 16 rows; on 10 of them it is below one 16-bit step, so no jump
remains in the written file. Resynthesising the stitched recording with a neural vocoder was
rejected, because it would regenerate all of the speaker's real audio, not just the junctions.

In the target pauses, $\bar{T}_{p}$ is the median gap between voiced stretches of the
utterance, clamped to $[0.04, 0.40]$\,s (measured speakers range from 40 to 400\,ms), and
$s_g{=}1$. The class scales $(s_{\mathrm{pre}}^{(c)}, s_{\mathrm{post}}^{(c)})$ are laughter
$(0.6, 1.4)$, sigh $(0.5, 1.2)$, cough and throat clearing $(0.7, 1.0)$, sneeze $(0.8, 1.3)$,
sniff $(0.5, 0.7)$, and $(1, 1)$ for the other classes.
% NOTE: the earlier draft said these scales were measured from the pauses around each tag. In
% the code (Para/para_synth/vocalsound.py, GAP_SHAPE) they are priors set from this
% respiratory reasoning, not measurements, and only the six VocalSound classes have them.
% Measure them, or keep this wording.
The inserted silence is $\max(0, T - T_{\mathrm{exist}})$, where $T_{\mathrm{exist}}$ is the
silence already on that side of the cut. Because the cut sits in the middle of a measured pause,
each side already has 0.30--0.53\,s of silence on the pilot, so silence was added before the
event on only 1 of 16 rows and after it on 7. It is filled with the recording's quietest
100\,ms, tiled with crossfaded seams. Generated white noise was tried first and audibly hissed:
in a test recording whose background carries 1.5\% of its energy above 2\,kHz, the generated
noise carried 82\%, against 1.3\% for the tiled background. Finally the whole recording is
peak-normalised to 0.95 and written as 16-bit PCM at 22.05\,kHz.

\subsection{Filter}
NISQA~\citep{nisqa} is used because a spliced recording has no clean reference to compare
against, and NISQA predicts a listening panel's MOS, and separate quality dimensions such as
discontinuity, from one recording alone. A row is kept if (1)~its NISQA MOS drops by
${\le}\,0.5$ from source to result, (2)~its NISQA discontinuity drops by ${\le}\,0.22$, catching
an audible seam at the splice, and (3)~its boundary activity at the cut is ${\le}\,1.2$.
Boundary activity is measured on the source recording as the RMS of the 50\,ms on each side of
the cut divided by the RMS of that whole side (0 means the cut is in silence, about 1 means it
is in speech of average loudness), and a row is judged by its worse side.

The thresholds were set on the pilot. The MOS drop ranged from $-0.09$ to $+0.75$ (median
$+0.12$), and any threshold from 0.40 to 0.55 removes the same single outlier. The discontinuity
drop ranged from $-0.28$ to $+0.45$, with 14 of 16 rows at or below $+0.10$, so any threshold
from 0.10 to 0.23 keeps the same rows; 0.22 was chosen by listening and rejects 2 of 16. In all,
13 of 16 synthesised rows pass. Boundary activity on slot-placed rows is 0.002--0.056 (median
0.006), so the third check rejects nothing on this path; it guards input whose tag position was
chosen without regard to pauses, where 1.2 rejects 3 of 20 rows, 1.0 rejects 7 and 0.8 rejects
11, and where 2 of the 3 rows it rejects pass every NISQA threshold. NISQA scores are not stable
across renders: Seed-VC's diffusion is not seeded, and re-rendering the same 20 rows moved
discontinuity drops by up to 0.25, changing the kept count from 10 to 13. A rejection therefore
means that one conversion was bad, and re-rendering a rejected row may produce a passing take.

\section{Evaluation details}\label{app:eval}

\subsection{Test set}\label{app:test}
A tag fits a sentence when the sentence describes the situation that produces the sound: being
startled for a gasp, falling sound asleep for snoring, or pepper or bitter cold for a sneeze.
The tag sits after the action word or at a comma.
Of the 420 tags, 281 (67\%) are mid-sentence, 116 at the end and 23 at the start; throat
clearing leans towards the start of the sentence, and snoring, sniffing and screaming towards
the end. 92 sentences were lightly edited to fix spelling or cut a garbled ending. Sentences
are 7--48 words long (median 14) and contain almost no English words. 21 sentences contain
digits, which the reference keeps but the ASR writes out as words, so they count as errors:
without them, the released model's WER falls from 8.11\% to 6.94\%.

\subsection{Metric procedures}\label{app:metrics}
\textbf{NVPA and NVMR.} The tag is removed and the remaining words are force-aligned to the
16\,kHz output with a Vietnamese wav2vec~2.0 CTC model~\citep{wav2vec2}
(\nolinkurl{nguyenvulebinh/wav2vec2-base-vietnamese-250h}) and torchaudio's
\texttt{forced\_align}. Each word boundary is a slot, and the tag sits at one of them.
Qwen3-ForcedAligner is not used here: besides stretching words over non-verbal sounds
(Appendix~\ref{app:data}), on Vietnamese it collapses runs of words to one instant.
FlexSED~\citep{flexsed} then
scores each class prompt over the whole recording, in 10\,s windows with a 5\,s hop, and each
frame keeps its highest posterior across windows. A class's events are runs of frames above 0.2
that reach the class threshold at least once. The threshold is 0.5 for most classes, 0.4 for
throat clearing, and 0.7 for breathing, which FlexSED also scores at 0.5--0.7 on pauses where no
breath is heard. Runs less than 0.12\,s apart are merged, events shorter than 0.08\,s are
dropped, and events shorter than 0.25\,s that touch either end of the clip are ignored as
cut-off tails. A tag is \emph{correct} if an event of its class lies within 3\,s of its slot
with no aligned word in between; a word that overlaps the event by at most 0.05\,s does not
count as in between. Otherwise the tag is \emph{misplaced} if exactly one word separates the
nearest event of its class from the slot, \emph{wrong class} if only another class fires at the
slot, and \emph{missed} if no event of any class is at the slot. Classes are scored strictly: a
sigh, pant or gasp also triggers FlexSED's breathing prompt, but counts as correct only when its
own class fires.

% breathing were set by listening; the 0.5 of the other ten classes is a placeholder. Calibrate

\textbf{WER.} The ASR model is a Zipformer~\citep{zipformer} transducer,
\nolinkurl{sherpa-onnx-zipformer-vi-2025-04-20}, with greedy
search on the 16\,kHz output. The reference and the transcript are normalised alike: tags are
removed, the text is put in Unicode NFC and lower-cased, and every character other than a
letter or digit becomes a space. WER is computed at corpus level over all items of a system,
not as a mean of per-item WERs.

\textbf{SIM-o.} The speaker model is ECAPA-TDNN~\citep{ecapa},
\nolinkurl{speechbrain/spkrec-ecapa-voxceleb}. Both
recordings are embedded at 16\,kHz and the embeddings are L2-normalised (0--1 in practice).
